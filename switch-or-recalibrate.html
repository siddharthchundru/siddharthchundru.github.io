Switching, Recalibrating or Relearning?

Kinds of test-time adaptation in robot policies.

Research question

A pretrained robot policy is deployed, and something changes. If the world changes, for example when a route becomes blocked, sensory input still looks normal; what fails is the behaviour, and adapting means switching to a different behaviour the policy already has. If the body changes by degree, for example when actuators weaken or a sensor drifts, the relation between command and outcome shifts but keeps its direction, and adapting means recalibrating. If the body is reversed, for example when an actuator's direction is inverted, adapting may mean relearning: acquiring a new controller. Are these different problems, solved by different mechanisms?

The third kind comes from human motor learning, where mirror reversal is learned from scratch rather than adapted to, with no aftereffects once it is undone (Yang, Cowan & Haith, 2021). Recalibrating and relearning are told apart here by what the robot does: adjusting a controller in place leaves an aftereffect when the change is undone; storing a new controller alongside the old one, and selecting between them, does not.

Hypothesis

Different families of mechanism handle different kinds of change, and leave different behavioural signatures. Habit-like mechanisms, which keep a use-dependent memory of the robot's own behaviour, should persist with a strategy after it stops working and return to it readily once it works again. Homeostatic mechanisms, which restore internal and sensorimotor operating ranges, should recalibrate without any model of the error. Error-based mechanisms, the standard model of human motor adaptation, should recalibrate graded changes but push the wrong way under reversal. A mechanism that stores and reselects controllers should recover from reversal without an aftereffect. The habit and homeostatic families draw on embodied and artificial-life research (Egbert & Barandiaran, 2014; Di Paolo, 2000), here applied to modern pretrained policies.

Two tracks

  • Recalibrating and relearning after body changes (method). Homeostatic test-time adaptation: gated, local plasticity in a frozen pretrained policy, with no reward and no gradients. Each monitor changes only parameters that can affect what it monitors: units inside the policy restore their usual activation ranges, and a correction between policy and body is adjusted when intended and realised movement stop matching. Compared against error-based adaptation, online identification of the body, a store-and-select mechanism, and small analogues of current test-time adaptation methods.
  • Switching after world changes (evaluation). A controlled comparison of switching mechanisms, including online fine-tuning, nearest-neighbour retrieval, failure-avoidance resampling adapted from Du et al. (2024) and use-dependent habit memories, measured for how long they persist, how quickly they switch and whether they return once the route reopens.

How the robot adapts, not only whether

Recent test-time adaptation methods for robot policies report success after a change. This project also characterises how adaptation happens: aftereffects when a change is undone, faster adaptation on a second exposure, and the cost of adapting when nothing has changed. Each post-change trajectory is labelled by route and by movement pattern, computed from realised motion and compared with how an unadapted policy moves under the same change, giving four outcomes: recovered (old route, old movement), rerouted (new route, old movement), compensated (old route, new movement) and novel (new route, new movement).

Relation to other work

The base policies come from What Should a Robot Practise?, which produces policies whose repertoire differs in a controlled way. That makes it possible to test whether switching depends on how many alternative behaviours a policy has kept.
